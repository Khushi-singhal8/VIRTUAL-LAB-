To study joining of two metal sheets using the brazing process.

Theory

What is brazing?

Brazing is a method of joining metals using a filler material that melts above 450 °C, but lower than the melting point of the base metals. It’s joined by flowing into the joint via capillary action, without melting the base sheets themselves.

Applications

This process is found in automotive and HVAC systems for radiators and tubing, in electronics for joining delicate components, and even in aerospace and manufacturing for high-precision, complex assemblies.

Advantages:

  • Can join different metals (like copper to steel) and thin, intricate parts.
  • Uses lower heat, causing less distortion and preserving the original properties of the base metals.
  • Produces smooth, leak-proof joints, ideal for automated, high-volume workflows.

Disadvantages:

  • Joints are generally weaker than welded ones and not suitable for high-heat environments.
  • Requires meticulous cleaning and precise gap control to succeed.
  • Limited by the size of the parts—less practical for large workpieces.

Apparatus:

  • Two clean metal sheets (e.g., mild steel or copper).
  • Torch and gas setup (e.g., oxy-acetylene or propane).
  • Filler rods (brass, copper alloy, or silver alloy).
  • Flux (borax). Flux (borax).
  • Tools for surface prep: wire brush, file.
  • Clamps or fixtures.
  • Safety gear: gloves, goggles, apron.

Precaution:

  • Clean surfaces thoroughly before brazing.
  • Maintain precise joint fits.
  • Avoid overheating base metals.
  • Use flux properly to avoid weak joints.
  • Cool joints naturally to prevent cracks.
